Language Models

Predicting the Next Token

How does an artificial intelligence model think?

We often ask ourselves: how is it possible that AI answers my questions? Do computers think like us? In my opinion, they think very differently from us. Basically, they think by brute force, they generate thousands of possibilities at once and choose the one that seems best, but they don't know why.

In this lesson, we're going to explore one of the many steps a model takes to generate its responses, specifically, how it chooses the answer.

01Tokens, not words

When you type a prompt and press enter, a language model does not "look up" an answer or understand your question the way a person does. It does something much simpler, repeated very fast: it predicts the next piece of text, one piece at a time.

First, the model breaks your text into tokens, small chunks that are often a word, part of a word, or a punctuation mark. The word "cat" might be one token; a longer word like "unbelievable" might be split into several ("un", "believ", "able"). Spaces and punctuation count too. The model only ever sees tokens, never letters or "meaning" the way we do.

Then it does one thing over and over: given all the tokens so far, guess the most likely next token. It adds that token to the end, then feeds the whole thing back in and guesses again.

"The sky is" → "blue".
"The sky is blue and" → "the".

One token at a time, the response builds up.

02Probabilities and temperature

The model isn't choosing from a fixed answer. For each step it produces a ranked list of possible next tokens with probabilities, maybe "blue" 71%, "clear" 12%, "grey" 6%, and so on. Usually it leans toward the likely ones, but it doesn't always pick the very top one. A setting often called temperature controls how adventurous it is: low temperature almost always takes the safest, highest-probability token; higher temperature lets it reach for less likely ones. That is why the exact same prompt can produce different answers on different runs, there is a controlled amount of randomness in which token gets picked.

    You probably got the same phrase every time at such a low temperature, right? With every token pushed close to 100%:

    I walked to the park.

  3. Now change the temperature to 2. This will make the probability values more evenly spread and increase the chance of a gibberish phrase. Try a couple of times to generate a phrase that doesn't make sense. Write them down and observe which values were chosen.

    After generating several outputs at temperature 2, you'll likely see gibberish sentences, such as:

    "I walked to Wednesday spaghetti." highest token at 20%

  4. After generating some sentences as an AI does, do you think an AI model can explain how it thought about what it said? Can it reflect on its own thinking?

    No, it can't reflect on its own thinking, since its thinking is just finding a suitable chain of words. And if you do ask the model about it, this won't track back the way we do about our thoughts. Instead, the AI will generate a totally new message.

The key idea: a language model generates text by repeatedly predicting the next token from a list of probabilities, it doesn't retrieve answers, it builds them one guess at a time.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Text is broken into tokens, not words or letters, and the model only sees tokens.
  • ✓The model works left to right, one token at a time, always predicting what comes next.
  • ✓Each prediction is a probability distribution over many possible next tokens, not a single fixed answer.
  • ✓Because the pick involves some randomness (temperature), the same prompt can give different results.
