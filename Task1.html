Learning Science

Spaced Repetition: Scientific Findings for an Iron Memory

One of the most reliable findings in learning science: when you review matters as much as how much.

Here's what that means and how to use it starting today.

We all know that memory fades over time. Things you don't practice are quickly forgotten. But over a century ago, the psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus decided to study how we forget. In this lesson, you'll learn about his conclusions and discover how to use them to optimize your learning. The secret isn't just about reviewing, it's about timing. We call this spaced repetition.

01The forgetting curve

The first concept Ebbinghaus introduced was the Forgetting Curve, which he illustrated with a graph.

This graph is straightforward. The vertical axis represents the probability that you can remember a specific piece of information, like a concept or a vocabulary word. For example, imagine you just learned to say "good morning" in Hungarian: jo reggelt.

Right after you learn it and can recall it on your own, that's represented as 100% on the scale. But this only counts if you can remember and reproduce it, not just recognize it. Seeing jo reggelt and knowing what it means doesn't count. You have to be able to say jo reggelt from memory.

The graph shows that the probability of remembering decreases over time, but the rate of forgetting isn't constant. You forget very quickly at first, and then the forgetting slows down.

  1. How much has the probability of remembering dropped 24 hours after learning?
    Retention drops from 100% on day 0 to about 51% on day 1. That means you lose roughly 49 percentage points, nearly half of everything you learned vanishes in just the first 24 hours.
  2. Why is it a bad idea to cram for a vocabulary exam the night before?
    By the time you get to the exam, you'll be lucky to remember half of the information, your memory simply doesn't work that way. Without reviews to slow the decay, most of what you crammed will have already faded.
  3. How much probability of remembering remains 5 days after learning? Is that reliable learning?
    Only about 28% remains on day 5. That means more than two-thirds of the material has already been forgotten. No, that's not reliable learning, it's a shaky foundation at best, and you'd be relying on luck rather than memory.
  4. Change the graph to a straight line. Looking at the pink one, what is the probability of remembering 24 hours after learning? And after the 3rd and 4th day?
    Always 12%. No matter which day, on a straight line, it is always constant. This is why discovering that our memory retention follows a curve was such an important finding by Hermann Ebbinghaus.

It might seem like a discouraging picture, if it's this hard to remember things, how do we learn anything at all? But Ebbinghaus had another crucial finding.

02Spaced repetition

Repetition strengthens our brain, just like it strengthens our muscles. Ebbinghaus's data proves it.

When you review knowledge, your brain strengthens the neural structures you built the first time. If you remember it today and use it, you'll know it just as well as before, but stronger. We can represent this with a new forgetting curve. This new curve is similar to the first, starting at 100% on the day you review, but it's slightly more horizontal and sits above the previous one.

Now, observe the graph. We start on Day 0, having learned a concept, with the red curve. Now, move the slider to Day 2. Upon reviewing, a new orange line appears, and the red one becomes a dashed line below it. The orange curve shows that your learning has improved because you reviewed. The second curve fades over time, but more slowly than the first. That's the power of repetition.

  1. What do the new colored lines mean? And why are some of them dashed?
    Each new colored line represents a new revision of the same concept, it means the brain has triggered the circuit that prompts the concept. The previous lines become dashed because they represent how retention would look if we never revised after that point.
  2. What two things about the shape of the lines change with each new revision?

    1. The curvature of the graph: the red line has a more pronounced drop, it decays more quickly than the blue line, which looks like it's "waking up."

    2. The height at which the lines flatten out: each new revision places the flat part higher than the previous one, we can see this thanks to the dashed tails.

03Optimized repetition

So, how can we use these findings to improve our learning? The optimal time to review is when your retention is around 50%. Looking at the graph above, you can see that forgetting takes longer with each review. That's why the recommendation is to review soon after learning, and then double the gap before each subsequent review session.

The key idea: Memory fades fast at first and then more and more slowly. Reviewing right around the moment you would otherwise forget, and spacing each subsequent review further apart, is what turns a fragile first impression into an iron memory.

Key takeaways

  • ✓The Forgetting Curve is not a straight line. Retention drops fastest right after learning, then keeps fading more and more slowly.
  • ✓Every review reshapes the curve. It strengthens what you knew, and the new curve decays more slowly than the last one.
  • ✓The best moment to review is around 50% retention. Not as soon as you learn it, and not once it's already gone.
  • ✓The 50% point moves further away each time. Therefore, an efficient review calendar doubles the gap after every review: Day 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32…
